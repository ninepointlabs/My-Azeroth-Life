A Gnome Called Father

The snow in Coldridge Valley does not fall so much as it settles in, like a dwarf at a bar who has no intention of leaving before closing. Into that snow walked a gnome priest who answers to the name Father, which is a great deal of name for someone who comes up roughly to a dwarf’s belt buckle. The chronicler has seen grander titles worn by smaller men. Not many, but some.

Dun Morogh is a cold place to begin a life. The wind comes down off the peaks with nothing to stop it, the wolves are thin and opinionated, and the dwarves of Anvilmar greet every newcomer with the same expression: polite, skeptical, already calculating how long you will last. Father did not seem to mind. Gnomes rarely do. They were driven out of their own city and have been walking into worse places with a cheerful look ever since.

His first errand was the most honest work in the valley. The Dwarven Outfitters needed supplies, and nobody in Coldridge has ever accused a dwarven outfitter of being sentimental about where those supplies come from. Father went out among the wolves with his staff and his small, earnest prayers and came back with what was asked. Somewhere in the middle of it he felt the first faint stirring of strength, the kind that comes from surviving a morning that could have gone otherwise. It is the smallest rung on a very long ladder. He climbed it anyway.

The outfitters paid him in thanks. Before he had finished brushing the snow from his robes, the valley handed him three more burdens in the space of a dozen heartbeats: a satchel of Coldridge Valley mail that needed carrying, a sealed letter called the Hallowed Memorandum, and word of A New Threat stirring in the hills. This is how it goes in Dun Morogh. You do one small kindness and the mountains decide you are the sort who does kindnesses.

The Memorandum took him inside Anvilmar, out of the wind, into the warm stone halls where the dwarves keep their fires and their teachers. He delivered it, and in the delivering found himself handed a startling amount of learning for such a short visit. A pick and the knowledge of where ore hides in the rock. The craft of smelting copper from it. The herb-lore of finding what grows in cold places, and something the teachers simply called gardening, which the chronicler suspects is what dwarves say when they mean “keeping anything alive up here.” He paid forty coppers for the privilege and did not haggle. A priest who can mine, smelt, gather herbs and tend a garden is either remarkably well-rounded or has not yet been told which of these things he will be bad at.

And last, quietly, the thing that mattered most: the Power Word: Fortitude, a prayer that lays the strength of the Light over another’s shoulders like a borrowed coat. It is a small blessing, as blessings go. It is also the first thing a priest learns that is entirely for someone else. The chronicler finds that telling.

Thirteen minutes, all told. He walked out of Anvilmar a little stronger, eighty-two coppers richer and forty poorer, which in Dun Morogh counts as breaking even with dignity.

The mail is still in his satchel, undelivered. Out in the hills, the new threat is still nameless and still waiting. The wolves have not forgotten him. And somewhere in the valley there is surely someone who will look down at this small figure in snow-dusted robes, hear him introduce himself, and ask the question every gnome named Father must eventually answer.

Father of what, exactly?
