The Tauren and the Battleboar

The first thing Gandan did in this world was accept a quest. The second thing he did was accept another quest. The third thing he did was accept a third quest. The chronicler, watching from the edge of Camp Narache with a hand wrapped around a mug he definitely did not pay for, recognized the type immediately: the over-committer. The one who will drown in his own quest log before he reaches level ten.

Gandan is a tauren druid, broad-shouldered and steady-hoofed, born on the red mesa of Mulgore under a sky so wide it makes even the kodo look small. He took his first steps with the same quiet dignity the tauren bring to everything, which is to say he immediately began killing things for their pelts. The Hunt Begins, the quest-giver called it. The hunt never ends, the chronicler would add, but that comes later.

He was efficient. The chronicler must give him that. “A Humble Task” sent him to fetch water from a well, which sounds pastoral until you learn the well is guarded by cougars who do not share the tauren philosophy of peaceful coexistence. He fetched the water anyway, delivered it to an old crone who rewarded him with seventeen copper and a follow-up quest, because there is always a follow-up quest. This one sent him across Red Cloud Mesa to deliver more water, as if the first trip had not been lesson enough about the cougars.

By the time the sun climbed to its peak, Gandan had learned to skin a beast, to pick an herb, and to find herbs he had not yet picked. He learned these things from instructors who all said the same thing: this will pay for itself. The chronicler has heard this from every tradeskill trainer on three continents and can confirm it is technically true if you ignore the time investment, the death runs, and the goblin auction-house fees.

At level two he killed enough plainstriders to complete The Hunt Begins. At level three he had moved on to mountain cougars for The Hunt Continues, because the naming convention in Mulgore is not subtle. A tauren shaman handed him a Verdant Note, which is the druid equivalent of a hall pass, and sent him to a druid trainer who taught him Mark of the Wild and looked at him with the expression of someone who has taught Mark of the Wild to ten thousand young tauren and will teach it to ten thousand more.

Brambleblade Ravine is where Gandan met his first real test. The quilboar there are not the gentle sort; they are the kind who have named their ravine “Brambleblade” and consider that a selling point. He went in for the Rite of Strength and came out at level four with blood on his hooves that was not his own. The Earthmother’s rites are not ceremonial. They never were.

He learned Moonfire in the ravine. He learned Rejuvenation shortly after, because the two go together like meat and bandages. A druid who cannot heal himself is a druid who will spend a lot of time walking back from the graveyard.

The afternoon brought Break Sharptusk!, a quest whose exclamation mark should be the first warning. Sharptusk is a battleboar with a name that suggests the tauren have been dealing with him for a very long time. Gandan tracked him, fought him, and won, which earned him five levels on the day and a quest journal that overflowed with things left undone. The Adventurer quest sat in his log like a promise. Rites of the Earthmother awaited the next step. Somewhere out on the mesa, the battleboars were breeding replacements.

He logged out at level five with seventy copper more than he started with, which is profit in the strictest sense and poverty in every other. The chronicler watched him fade into the inn’s shadows and made a note: this one finishes what he starts. The quest log will not defeat him. The cougars might.
